An Examination of Tarkington ISD Board Members' Land Holdings, Municipal Utility District Connections, and Central Appraisal District Ties in Liberty County
I. Executive Summary
This report presents a comprehensive investigation into the land ownership, land flipping activities, and connections to Municipal Utility Districts (MUDs) and the Central Appraisal District (CAD) of specific Tarkington Independent School District (TISD) board members. The inquiry focuses on Susan Rollins, Dwayne Stovall, and Lane Gulledge, alongside Kem Kirkham-Arnold (a former board member) and Rollins' husband, Jimmy Rollins. The individual named "Cory Anderson Burton" in the query is clarified as two distinct individuals, Cory Anderson and Donald Burton.
The investigation reveals a complex and often opaque network of relationships and potential conflicts of interest within Liberty County's rapidly expanding development landscape. While direct evidence of "land flipping" by the named TISD board members is not explicitly detailed in the provided information, their roles and the activities of their close associates intersect with broader county-wide issues. These issues include large-scale land development, MUD operations characterized by questionable bond elections and alleged misuse of funds, and a pervasive lack of public oversight. Notably, Jimmy Rollins, husband of TISD Trustee Susan Rollins, is confirmed to be a member of the Liberty County CAD Board of Directors, a position that directly influences property valuation. MUD 1 and MUD 15 are central to significant controversies involving developer control, opaque financial dealings, and alleged infrastructure deficiencies, which in turn place considerable strain on public services like Tarkington ISD.
The findings collectively indicate a systemic environment where rapid, often unregulated, development is closely intertwined with public and private interests. This confluence of roles and financial stakes may create conditions conducive to conflicts of interest and potential erosion of public trust, ultimately impacting taxpayers and the provision of essential public services.
II. Introduction: Context of Tarkington ISD and Liberty County Development
Tarkington Independent School District (TISD) Overview
Tarkington Independent School District is a public school district situated in the north-central region of Liberty County, Texas, spanning an area of 238 square miles. All four of its campuses are consolidated on a single plot of land, with the high school prominently positioned facing County Road 2268 and the other three campuses aligned along Farm to Market Road 163. Small portions of the city of Cleveland also fall within the district's geographical boundaries.
The governance of TISD is entrusted to a seven-member Board of Trustees. These individuals are elected at-large for three-year terms through non-partisan elections, with a rotating schedule ensuring that either two or three positions are subject to election annually. Current board members include Lane Gulledge, who serves as President; Kevin Johnson, the Vice President; Paige Bostwick, the Secretary; Susan Rollins, a Trustee; Dwayne Stovall, also a Trustee; and Jason Ward, another Trustee. Jackie Flores is additionally listed as a board member.
In response to a burgeoning student population, TISD is actively planning a substantial $95 million school bond initiative. This bond, scheduled for a May 2025 election, is designed to address critical infrastructure needs, including the acquisition of land and the construction of a new elementary school. Specifically, Proposition A of the bond proposes $50 million for campus and facility upgrades, while Proposition B allocates $45 million for the new elementary school and related land purchases. The district has engaged with over ten landowners in its pursuit of suitable property. As of the current reporting, no land has been formally purchased, and the precise purchase price remains confidential until a final agreement is executed, a measure intended to protect the district's bargaining position.
Rapid Development and MUD Controversies in Liberty County
Liberty County has experienced a period of explosive and largely uncontrolled growth, significantly driven by the expansion of Colony Ridge, a massive development that has nearly tripled in size from approximately 8,000 acres in 2019 to almost 20,000 acres currently. This rapid influx of residents has placed immense strain on existing public services, including both Cleveland Independent School District and Tarkington Independent School District, which are struggling to accommodate the increased student enrollment.
Municipal Utility Districts (MUDs) serve as a primary mechanism facilitating this development. These special-purpose districts are created by the state, empowering developers to fund the upfront costs of essential infrastructure, such as water, wastewater, drainage, and roads, particularly in unincorporated areas outside established city limits. A critical characteristic of MUDs is their governance by locally elected boards. These elections often experience extremely low voter turnout, sometimes involving only a handful of eligible residents, effectively allowing developers to control the composition of these boards and thereby influence the authorization of significant debt to be repaid by future property taxes. This structure raises fundamental questions about the democratic accountability of these entities.
The rapid development has been consistently associated with severe infrastructure deficiencies, most notably concerning drainage and flood control, leading to recurrent and widespread flooding incidents. Documented development practices include unregulated water rerouting and extensive clearcutting of land without adequate mitigation measures, with a notable absence of natural waterway restoration post-construction. These practices exacerbate flood risks for nearby residents.
Compounding these issues are widespread allegations of predatory lending practices, primarily targeting Hispanic homebuyers. These practices involve the sale of flood-prone land often lacking essential utilities, coupled with high-interest loans that frequently result in high foreclosure rates. For example, Colony Ridge has reportedly reacquired approximately 45% of the over 35,000 properties it sold in the past decade, indicating a significant rate of foreclosures. Furthermore, concerns about self-dealing contracts within MUDs, where developers appear to profit from the very boards intended to serve public needs, have been described by experts as "unethical and inappropriate". This arrangement allows private entities to leverage public funds for their own benefit, creating a complex and potentially compromised governance environment.

Liberty County is characterized by extensive land parcels, with rural land offerings ranging significantly in size, from approximately 2.51 to 89.1 acres. This cycle frequently involves shell entities such as BRD Land & Investment, SRC Holdings, and Colony Ridge. These transactions are often executed through $0 or $10 deeds, which can obscure the true nature of the transfer and the underlying financial arrangements. This widespread use of vendor liens and nominal-value deeds by developers and associated entities suggests a deliberate strategy to rapidly acquire, transfer, and re-sell properties, potentially exploiting legal loopholes and vulnerable buyers. The high foreclosure rates associated with these practices, with Colony Ridge reportedly reacquiring 45% of its sold properties and accounting for 92% of Liberty County foreclosures between 2017 and 2022, directly illustrate the consequences of these predatory lending schemes. V. Connections to Municipal Utility Districts (MUD 1 and MUD 15)
Municipal Utility Districts (MUDs) play a pivotal role in the rapid development of Liberty County, often serving as mechanisms for financing infrastructure in unincorporated areas. The operations of MUD 1 and MUD 15 reveal significant concerns regarding their formation, funding, and governance.
MUD 15: Formation, Funding, and Alleged Irregularities
Liberty County MUD 15 was authorized by voters in May 2025 following an election that included five propositions seeking approval for bonds totaling up to $88 million. These bonds were earmarked for critical infrastructure, including water, wastewater, roads, drainage, and initial operating expenses. The MUD's engineer estimated specific costs: $59.0 million for water/wastewater facilities and $29.36 million for roads.
A highly unusual and concerning aspect of MUD 15's formation was the election results themselves. The unofficial vote on each of the five bond propositions was reported as an exact tie, with 1 vote "for" and 1 "against". Similarly, five candidates ran for the five proposed district board seats (Hebert, Hines, Ankenbrandt, Parrott, Bowman), and each candidate received precisely 1 vote, representing 100% of the 5 total votes cast. The report explicitly notes that only two precinct votes were cast in total. This pattern of extremely low-turnout and seemingly pre-determined election results for MUD 15 is a significant indicator of potential manipulation. Such outcomes are characteristic of MUDs established in undeveloped areas where a developer can control the few eligible votes, effectively hand-picking the board and authorizing massive debt to be repaid by future residents. This raises serious questions about the democratic legitimacy of MUD 15's creation and its $88 million bond authorization, suggesting it was primarily a mechanism for developer-driven financing rather than a publicly vetted initiative. The lack of clear public hearings or resident representation prior to bond activity further supports this conclusion.
Further scrutiny of MUD 15 reveals specific permit details and developer ties. The wastewater permit (TCEQ #WQ0016736001) for "Tarkington WWTP #1" was initially filed by BRD Land & Investment and subsequently transferred to Liberty County MUD 15. Glenn Breisch of Wasteline Engineering is identified as the Engineer of Record for BRD/Wasteline on MUD 15 plats. The planned sludge disposal site for Tarkington WWTP #1 is Austin, not Liberty County, indicating a lack of localized waste management infrastructure within the county. The permit was filed with a blank co-permittee, raising suspicions of a shell entity intended for future use. Public notification for the MUD 15 permit was found to be inadequate, limited to a library notice with no door-to-door or public meetings held prior to its approval. The total known financial exposure linked to MUD 15's creation and its bond-backed infrastructure is estimated at $8.94 million.
MUD 1 (Colony Ridge): Developer Control and Self-Dealing
Liberty County Municipal Management District No. 1 (MUD 1) was established in late 2017 at the specific request of William "Trey" and John Harris, the developers of Colony Ridge Inc.. The district's governance is tightly controlled by a "close-knit group of family and friends" connected to Colony Ridge Inc.. John Harris serves as the district's board president, alongside his niece and three Colony Ridge employees. Despite legislative intent for Cleveland ISD and Liberty County Commissioners Court to appoint board members, public records indicate these appointments never occurred, allowing the developer to maintain full control over the board's composition.
MUD 1 has approved $22.5 million in contracts for Liberty Paving, a company indirectly owned by the Harris brothers and another board member. Meeting minutes show that the family-controlled board unanimously approved two contracts totaling $16 million for Liberty Paving between 2022 and 2023, and a third contract for $6.5 million in November 2024. John Harris, who owns at least 10% of Liberty Paving, abstained from voting on the third contract but voted on the initial two. Experts in municipal government have characterized this arrangement as "unethical" and "inappropriate," emphasizing that a management district and its developer should operate as entirely separate entities. The governance structure of MUD 1, where the developer (Harris family/Colony Ridge) effectively controls the public taxing district that awards multi-million dollar contracts to their own subsidiaries (Liberty Paving), represents a profound conflict of interest. This mechanism allows developers to use public bond funds to finance infrastructure built by their own companies, essentially reimbursing themselves with taxpayer money. This model diverts public funds into private hands, blurring the lines of accountability and potentially inflating costs without genuine competitive bidding. The reliance of local volunteer services on developer "largesse," such as the Harris family's $360,000 donation to a volunteer fire department , further illustrates how private influence can permeate public functions.
In September 2024, MUD 1 also entered into a cost-sharing agreement with the development's property owners association (also controlled by John Harris) to help fund patrols by the county sheriff's department. The district contributed nearly $440,000 towards this effort in November 2024.

VII. Broader Context: Allegations of Corruption and Lack of Oversight in Liberty County
The investigation into Tarkington ISD board members and their connections is situated within a broader landscape of alleged corruption and systemic issues in Liberty County, particularly concerning land development and public oversight.
Predatory Lending and Foreclosure Mill Business Model
Colony Ridge's core business model is characterized by federal and state authorities as a "foreclosure mill". This model reportedly targets foreign-born and Hispanic consumers, many of whom have limited or no access to traditional credit, by promising "cheap, ready-to-build land" and financing without requiring proof of income.
Developers allegedly make false representations about property conditions, such as the immediate availability of sewer, water, and electricity, leading buyers to incur substantial unanticipated expenses after closing. These misrepresented conditions often preclude buyers from making practical use of the land, resulting in exceptionally high default rates on seller-financed loans. Upon default, Colony Ridge reportedly forecloses, re-possesses the land without financial loss, and then resells the same plots to other unsuspecting buyers, sometimes at significantly inflated prices, thereby perpetuating a cycle of exploitation.
The scale of this operation is substantial: Colony Ridge reportedly reacquired approximately 45% of the over 35,000 properties it sold in the past decade. Federal investigators found that Colony Ridge accounted for 92% of foreclosures in Liberty County between 2017 and 2022. The alleged predatory lending practices by Colony Ridge are not merely isolated incidents but appear to be a systemic business model designed to churn properties through repeated foreclosures, generating significant profits for the developers. This exploitation of vulnerable, often non-English speaking, populations highlights a severe lack of consumer protection within the rapid development. The lawsuits filed by the U.S. Department of Justice and the Texas Attorney General underscore the severity and alleged illegality of these practices, indicating a failure of market mechanisms to self-regulate and protect consumers.
Infrastructure Failures and Environmental Concerns
The rapid expansion of Colony Ridge has severely outpaced infrastructure development, leading to chronic and widespread drainage and flood control issues. This has resulted in repeated flooding of homes and roads, notably the washout of FM1010.
Development practices include unregulated water rerouting, extensive clearcutting of land without mitigation, and a lack of documented natural waterway restoration, all of which exacerbate flood risks for nearby residents. Investigations by the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) confirmed numerous environmental violations, including erosion, uncontrolled silt flow into ditches and streams, and unstabilized drainage channels. Water samples showed elevated levels of dissolved and suspended solids, indicative of significant environmental degradation. Serious public health concerns also arise from documented sewage releases (48,000 gallons) and significant fecal contamination in creeks, with allegations of persistent leaks and cover-ups. The recurring infrastructure and environmental failures are a direct consequence of a development model that prioritizes rapid land clearing and construction over sustainable planning, environmental protection, and public safety. The alleged "war on wetlands" and reliance on less efficient in-ditch detention demonstrate a systemic disregard for long-term ecological and community impacts. The persistence of these issues, despite TCEQ investigations and lawsuits , points to a severe lack of effective regulatory enforcement and accountability from both developers and local officials.

VIII. Conclusion
The investigation into Tarkington ISD board members and their broader connections reveals a complex and often opaque landscape of relationships within Liberty County's rapid development. While direct evidence of large-scale "land flipping" by the named TISD board members is not explicitly detailed in the provided information, their public roles intersect with a system where land transactions, MUD operations, and public services are deeply intertwined with private interests.
Jimmy Rollins' confirmed position on the Liberty County CAD Board of Directors , coupled with his wife Susan Rollins' role as a TISD Trustee involved in land acquisition efforts , highlights a potential for indirect influence over property valuations and public land decisions. His active participation in CAD financial decisions, including waiving penalties and interest , demonstrates direct influence over tax-related matters, which are crucial for both public revenue and developer costs in a rapidly growing county. This confluence of roles, alongside his extensive background in agricultural land use, suggests a vested interest in property valuation policies that could intersect with public responsibilities.
The broader context of Liberty County's development reveals systemic challenges. The formation and funding of MUDs, particularly MUD 15, exhibit concerning irregularities such as extremely low-turnout elections that appear to facilitate developer control over significant public bond authorizations. MUD 1 (Colony Ridge) exemplifies a model where developers effectively control public taxing districts and award multi-million dollar contracts to their own subsidiaries, a practice deemed "unethical and inappropriate" by experts. This arrangement allows developers to leverage public funds for private gain, raising concerns about accountability and competitive fairness.
Furthermore, the prevalence of alleged predatory lending practices, characterized by high-interest loans, false promises of utilities, and subsequent high foreclosure rates, indicates a business model that exploits vulnerable populations for profit. These practices, alongside chronic infrastructure failures, environmental violations (e.g., sewage contamination, uncontrolled erosion), and a documented lack of regulatory enforcement, point to a development paradigm that prioritizes rapid expansion over sustainable planning and public welfare.
The investigation also uncovers a network of familial and professional interconnections among other key public officials, including the District Attorney and Constable, with private real estate entities. These relationships, coupled with a reported lack of recusal and the suppression of investigations, suggest a permissive environment for conflicts of interest and a lack of transparency in governance. The alleged manipulation of local media further compounds the issue, limiting public awareness and independent scrutiny of these complex dealings.
Collectively, the findings paint a picture of a rapidly developing county where the lines between public service and private financial interests are blurred. While direct evidence of "land flipping" by the named TISD board members is not explicitly established, their roles operate within a broader ecosystem characterized by significant financial, ethical, and environmental controversies related to land development and public oversight. The interconnectedness of these issues suggests that addressing them effectively would require a comprehensive approach to governance, transparency, and regulatory enforcement across multiple county departments and public entities.
